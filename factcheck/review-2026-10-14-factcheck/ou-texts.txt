### Sun [OU 402]
The OU studies how the solar wind interacts with Earth's magnetic field through the ESA–CAS SMILE mission, whose X-ray camera detectors were developed and tested at the OU's Centre for Electronic Imaging. How particles from the Sun damage the cameras and detectors on spacecraft is a core research area for the Centre, which tests detectors against space radiation for missions across the Solar System.
### Mercury [OU 382]
OU scientists have spent decades studying Mercury’s surface composition to understand the early volcanic history of the planet. Researchers use advanced spectroscopy to interpret data from visiting spacecraft and map the distribution of chemical elements. OU scientists are Co-Investigators on BepiColombo's MIXS X-ray spectrometer, which will map the elements on Mercury's surface.
### BepiColombo [OU 541]
OU academics were members of BepiColombo's M-CAM monitoring camera team, analysing images returned during the spacecraft's Mercury flybys, and have led ESA's Mercury Surface and Composition Working Group. The OU was also a development partner, alongside instrument lead the University of Leicester, in building the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer (MIXS). OU scientists are now Co-Investigators on MIXS and on MERTIS (the Mercury Thermal Infrared Spectrometer), and associates on SIMBIO-SYS (the orbiter's spectrometer and imaging system).
### Venus [OU 316]
The OU is helping to prepare ESA's EnVision mission to Venus. With a £2.3 million ESA grant, an OU team leads the testing and characterisation of the detectors in the VenSpec-U ultraviolet spectrometer, which will study the sulphur chemistry of Venus's clouds, and takes part in science preparations for the mission.
### Earth [OU 399]
The OU uses Earth observation data to monitor environmental changes and support international climate conservation efforts. Academics develop machine learning algorithms to analyse satellite imagery for tracking deforestation, urban growth, and glacial retreat. This research provides a critical foundation for teaching students about the intersection of technology and environmental sustainability.
### ISS [OU 473]
OU scientists took part in the DLR-led BIOlogy and Mars Experiment (BIOMEX) aboard the ISS's EXPOSE-R2 facility, mounted outside the Zvezda module, exposing bacteria, algae, lichens and fungi to the vacuum, UV radiation and temperature extremes of space for about 18 months. The samples returned to Earth with British astronaut Tim Peake in June 2016, and results helped assess whether such organisms (and, by extension, life on Mars) could survive interplanetary transfer.
### Hubble [OU 430]
OU astronomers actively use Hubble's extensive archive to research stellar populations and the evolution of distant galaxies, work that also feeds directly into the university's astronomy curriculum. The high-performance CCDs in Hubble's WFC3 camera are built by Teledyne e2v, the same UK manufacturer that partners closely with the OU's Centre for Electronic Imaging on the characterisation and radiation-hardening of space CCDs.
### CASTOR [OU 464]
The CEI at the OU was awarded £2.1 million to lead testing and space-qualification of the CIS303 and CIS120 UV detectors (built by Teledyne e2v) for CASTOR, a Canadian-led 1-metre UV/optical space telescope targeting launch around 2030. This includes electro-optical testing, optimising the detectors' performance, and measuring how well different UV coatings and enhancement technologies work and how they degrade, to raise the sensors' readiness for spaceflight.
### TRUTHS [OU 449]
The CEI carries out experimental radiation-damage characterisation of the Teledyne e2v CIS120 CMOS sensor being evaluated for TRUTHS and other next-generation missions, testing dark current, noise and quantum efficiency both before and after gamma and proton irradiation across a range of operating temperatures. This work helps confirm the detector technology can maintain the high calibration accuracy TRUTHS needs throughout a multi-year mission.
### SMILE [OU 445]
The Centre for Electronic Imaging developed and tested the twin CCD370 detectors (4510×4510-pixel, 18-micron-pixel sensors derived from the CCD270s developed for PLATO) that form the focal plane of SMILE's Soft X-ray Imager (SXI), running noise, charge-transfer-inefficiency and defect-mapping tests before flight. The OU is now part of the mission's operations team, working closely with the SXI team to get the most science from the detectors.
### UKube-1 [OU 558]
The CEI's Compact CMOS Camera Demonstrator (C3D) flew on UKube-1, the UK Space Agency's first CubeSat, after CEI won one of only three science payload slots. Launched in July 2014, its three cameras achieved the first in-orbit demonstration of a European 0.18-micron CMOS imaging technology, and radiation testing showed the sensor suffered dark-current and flatband-voltage degradation similar to CCDs while also revealing CMOS-specific effects like single-event upsets. This data has since informed the design of more radiation-tolerant space CMOS sensors.
### AlSat-Nano [OU 253]
The OU provided the C3D2 camera payload, based on the C3D camera flown on UKube-1. It took pictures of the Earth's surface while advancing the technology readiness of commercial camera sensors, and also monitored the CubeSat's deployable AstroTube boom.
### Moon [OU 548]
OU analysis of rock samples returned by the Apollo missions helped show that the Moon is not as dry as once thought, and that water is locked inside lunar minerals. Building on instrument experience from missions such as Beagle 2 and Rosetta, OU teams now develop miniature laboratories to measure what the Moon is made of. They also research in-situ resource utilisation: how lunar regolith could supply water, oxygen and fuel, and how it could be used to build on the Moon. This work supports plans for sustainable future exploration of the Moon.
### Chandrayaan-1 [OU 446]
The Centre for Electronic Imaging performed the proton radiation-damage assessment for the C1XS instrument's 24 CCD54 X-ray detectors, using ESA's SPENVIS software to model shielding, operating temperature, and voltage requirements. The team pre-flight characterised 14 candidate detector modules and recommended the ten best-performing units for flight, and continues to provide advisory support on the detectors' in-orbit radiation performance.
### Chang'e-5 [OU 420]
In April 2025 the OU was one of only seven institutions outside China selected to borrow samples from the Chang'e-5 mission. The OU team is using the university's laboratory facilities to determine the isotopic composition of carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and noble gases within the grains. This research aims to test the giant-impact theory of the Moon's origin and reveal the history of volatiles in the Earth-Moon system.
### PROSPECT [OU 404]
The OU is leading the design and development of ProSPA, a miniature laboratory to analyse samples in situ on the Moon. The instrument heats and processes lunar soil in the same way that meteorites and returned samples are analysed in laboratories on Earth. Its mass spectrometers identify, quantify and isotopically characterise water ice and other volatiles of high interest for science and exploration.
### LUPEX [OU 544]
The OU is developing the Exospheric Mass Spectrometer for LUPEX (EMS-L), an ESA instrument for the JAXA-ISRO Lunar Polar Exploration (LUPEX) rover mission, currently targeted for launch no earlier than 2028. Building on the OU's heritage in miniature ion-trap mass spectrometry, EMS-L will measure water and other volatile species in the lunar exosphere and those released from the surface and subsurface during rover and drilling activities, helping to characterise the distribution and behaviour of volatiles in the Moon's south polar region.
### MAGPIE [OU 392]
The OU is contracted to develop the Volatile Analyser (VA), a key element of the Lunar Volatiles Science (LVS) payload for ESA's MAGPIE rover. Building on the OU's heritage in ion-trap mass spectrometry, the instrument will analyse lunar material to identify and measure water and other volatiles, helping to characterise their abundance and how they vary with depth at the Moon's south pole.
### Shackleton Rover [OU 179]
OU scientists are involved in defining the payload and the mission for the Shackleton Rover, a concept for an entirely UK-built and operated rover system for the lunar south pole.
### Earth-Sun L2 [OU 392]
The OU uses this reference point as a virtual laboratory to study how deep-space environment conditions affect various satellite components. Academics monitor the performance of multiple OU-characterised missions that are stationed at this critical vantage point. This data is used to improve the design of future detectors for flagship missions currently under development at the university.
### JWST [OU 361]
OU astronomers use JWST to study the atmospheres of planets around other stars. They were part of the international teams that analysed JWST's first detailed observations of the giant planet WASP-39b, which revealed its clouds and chemistry, and OU-funded research has studied the small rocky planet L 98-59 d, where JWST found possible signs of volcanic gases.
### Gaia [OU 829]
The CEI modelled the charge-trapping effects of radiation damage in Gaia's CCD detectors ahead of launch, and has since analysed in-flight data from the BP/RP and RVS radiation campaigns, feeding directly into the mission's data-processing pipeline. In-orbit measurements showed Gaia's detectors accumulating less radiation damage than ground-based testing had predicted, a discrepancy the CEI is still investigating to improve how future missions are radiation-tested before launch. OU PhD students separately use Gaia's vast catalogues to study stellar clusters and binary star systems, and OU researchers use Gaia's science alerts to find transient objects. Gaia is a core case study in the OU's S818 Space Science module, where students use Gaia data to make follow-up observations with the OU's remote telescope in Tenerife.
### Euclid [OU 687]
The CEI played a lead role in characterising the CCD273 detectors for the VIS instrument and developed 'trap pumping', now used in orbit: a method that pinpoints individual radiation-damage defects in the detectors with sub-pixel precision, giving an accurate measure of how the detectors degrade in space so that images can be corrected and kept at science quality. This OU-developed calibration method is essential for correcting the mission's imaging data and preserving the high-precision shape measurements needed to trace dark matter through gravitational lensing. OU astronomers also lead research groups that use Euclid's wide-field surveys to identify rare gravitational lenses.
### NewAthena [OU 427]
The CEI is working with institutions around the world on the design and experimental testing of graded-Z shielding for the mission's Wide Field Imager (WFI) instrument, layering high- and low-Z materials to block high-energy particles and the secondary radiation they produce. Researchers use the GEANT4 simulation toolkit, validated against real detector tests, to model how this shielding will perform in orbit before launch.
### HWO [OU 417]
The CEI is developing high-precision, low-noise UV and photon-counting detectors for HWO's extremely faint imaging tasks, building on the university's CASTOR mission heritage. The OU is also part of the UK consortium that received £3 million in 2026 to develop one of the telescope's core instruments. This long-term research keeps the OU at the forefront of the international search for life beyond our solar system.
### PLATO [OU 547]
OU researchers lead population studies to identify and filter out astrophysical false positives (such as blended eclipsing binaries) that could otherwise be mistaken for planets in PLATO's target fields, work supported by UK Space Agency funding for the mission's Development Phase. The team also contributes detector performance, calibration, and radiation-tolerance studies through the university's Centre for Electronic Imaging. The OU's own robotic telescopes could provide follow-up observations of candidate stars once the mission is flying.
### Roman [OU 437]
CEI researchers at the OU worked under contract to JPL from 2014 to 2019 to characterise, design and test the EM-CCD sensor for Roman's coronagraph instrument, resulting in the CCD311, a customised, radiation-hardened variant of the CCD201 selected for flight. The team continues to refine the 'trap-pumping' calibration techniques developed for Euclid to mitigate the effects of cosmic radiation on these sensors throughout the mission.
### Mars [OU 392]
The OU has a long history of Martian research, ranging from the design of lander instruments to the analysis of orbital atmospheric data. Academics lead research into the planet's volatile cycles and the potential habitability of its subsurface environments. This research is used to support international space agencies in selecting landing sites for future rover and sample return missions.
### ExoMars TGO [OU 710]
The OU designed and led the development of the UVIS ultraviolet channel of the NOMAD spectrometer suite, and an OU planetary scientist serves as a Co-Principal Investigator for NOMAD. The OU also helps operate the CaSSIS stereo imager aboard ExoMars TGO. Researchers at the university use the UVIS data to track ozone, water vapour, and trace gases including methane in the Martian atmosphere, helping to understand how water escapes into space. Some measurements are timed with other spacecraft: TGO measures methane from orbit while the Curiosity rover measures it on the ground. This work involves international collaboration to validate atmospheric models and support the search for life on the Red Planet.
### Schiaparelli [OU 299]
The OU was part of the team behind DREAMS, Schiaparelli's suite of environmental sensors designed to measure the weather at the surface of Mars. OU scientists were also heavily involved in analysing the entry, descent and landing data to work out the state of the Martian atmosphere when it arrived.
### I-MIM [OU 245]
The OU is leading the analysis of the CHROMA-D detector for imaging the surface of Mars on the I-MIM mission. OU researchers are characterising the detector's performance and its sensitivity to radiation, to assess how well it suits the mission.
### Beagle 2 [OU 680]
The Beagle 2 mission was conceived and led by an OU professor, who designed the lander's Gas Analysis Package (GAP): a miniaturised, twelve-oven stepped-combustion mass spectrometer built to detect isotopic signatures of past life in Martian soil. Fitting a laboratory-scale instrument into a probe the size of a dinner plate required major feats of miniaturisation. The OU also led the Environmental Sensor Suite (ESS), designed to measure the weather at the surface of Mars and the presence of dust devils. Though contact with the lander was lost after its 2003 touchdown, the technical lessons learned went on to inform later international planetary landers, including ExoMars.
### Perseverance [OU 519]
OU researchers were part of the NASA/ESA Mars Sample Return Campaign Science Group and the planetary protection efforts, planning how best to return the samples collected by Perseverance in Jezero Crater and analyse them once they are back on Earth. OU researchers also use the rover's remote sensing and imaging data to study the mineralogy of the ancient river delta, including clay and carbonate minerals linked to past water activity, sharing this research with students through the OU's distance-learning platform.
### ExoMars Rosalind Franklin [OU 365]
OU researchers are members of the Rosalind Franklin rover's science team, serving as co-principal investigator for its PanCam camera system, as an Interdisciplinary Scientist, and more generally in support of PanCam and the Enfys spectrometer. OU researchers are also leading and contributing to mission preparations through operations simulations and field trials.
### MSL Curiosity [OU 474]
OU academics are members of the Mars Science Laboratory science and operations teams, focusing on noble-gas studies and the chemical interactions between Martian rocks and ancient water relevant to habitability. Other OU researchers combine the rover's atmospheric data with ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter observations to model Mars's dust and water cycles. This work is part of a broader international effort at the OU to understand the long-term habitability of the Red Planet.
### Europa [OU 648]
OU astrobiologists research Europa as one of the Solar System's most promising 'ocean worlds', developing novel cryo-analytical laboratory techniques and planetary simulation facilities to study icy surface materials at the molecular and micro-scale. Fieldwork in polar environments such as the Canadian High Arctic helps researchers understand how ice and salts delivered to the surface by cryovolcanism can reveal what conditions exist in the ocean far below. Researchers also model how the chemistry of Europa's ocean, and how habitable it is, may have changed over the moon's 4.5-billion-year history as the water reacted with the rock beneath.
### Ganymede [OU 158]
The OU's Centre for Electronic Imaging developed and tested the image sensor in the JANUS camera on ESA's JUICE mission, which will study Ganymede from orbit.
### JUICE [OU 343]
The OU's Centre for Electronic Imaging, working with detector manufacturer Teledyne e2v, developed and characterised the CIS115 CMOS image sensor at the heart of the JANUS camera system, testing it against the intense radiation, vibration and thermal extremes of the journey to Jupiter. The OU is the UK National Lead for the JANUS instrument.
### Saturn [OU 304]
OU researchers have studied dust in the Saturn system with Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyser, helping to calibrate the instrument and analysing the composition of dust around the planet. An OU scientist also led the Surface Science Package on the Huygens probe, which landed on Titan, Saturn's largest moon.
### Titan [OU 495]
Titan's first global geomorphological map, published in Nature Astronomy in 2019, used Cassini data to identify six major terrain types across the moon's surface, from plains to dune fields. Titan is also one of the icy 'ocean worlds' studied by OU astrobiologists, whose research explores how its surface chemistry and methane cycle could relate to conditions for life. This body-focused research sits alongside the OU's separate instrument-development work on the Cassini and Huygens missions.
### Cassini [OU 403]
OU planetary scientists used Cassini's extensive datasets to conduct world-leading research on the habitability and chemistry of Saturn's icy moons. OU researchers helped calibrate Cassini's Cosmic Dust Analyser and studied the composition of dust around Saturn. This research continues today as the university uses Cassini's archival data to train students in planetary science and data interpretation.
### Huygens [OU 345]
The OU was the Principal Investigator for the Surface Science Package (SSP), which recorded the first-ever in situ data from the surface of Titan, measuring its density, temperature and physical state on impact. OU academics have continued to lead analysis of this historic dataset to understand Titan's surface properties and organic chemistry.
### Rosetta [OU 464]
The OU co-developed the Ptolemy gas-analysis instrument on the Philae lander with RAL Space to measure the isotopic composition of cometary carbon, nitrogen and oxygen. OU scientists also contributed to the MUPUS surface-properties instrument and the GIADA dust analyser, and coordinated the mission's international ground-based observing campaign. Other OU research has explored why comet 67P's dust and gas jets follow a day-night cycle as it approaches the Sun.
### Philae [OU 436]
Together with RAL Space, the OU designed and built the Ptolemy gas analyser, which with the COSAC instrument made the first chemical analysis of the organic matter found on a comet's surface. Academics at the university lead the interpretation of this data to search for the chemical building blocks of life. This involvement has established the OU as a world leader in miniaturised planetary laboratory technology and cometary science.
### Hera [OU 64]
OU researchers were on the Hera Science Team's management board.
### AKARI [OU 487]
The OU was one of the international partner institutes on JAXA's AKARI infrared satellite, alongside Nagoya University, the University of Tokyo, ESA and other UK institutions. OU researchers used AKARI's all-sky infrared survey and near-infrared spectroscopy to study interstellar dust, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and the earliest stages of star formation in galaxies, work that helps build a clearer picture of the cosmic chemical cycle that eventually leads to habitable worlds.
### Spitzer [OU 386]
OU scientists analyse Spitzer's archival light curves to characterise the atmospheres and orbits of transiting exoplanets. Academics at the university use this data to teach students about infrared astronomy and the search for habitable worlds. This research remains a core component of the OU's astronomy research and is used to inform the target selection for new missions like Ariel.
### Herschel [OU 407]
The OU's astronomy group used data from SPIRE, the camera and spectrometer built for Herschel by an international consortium led by Cardiff University. Academics at the university lead research into sub-millimetre observations of distant galaxies to study the history of star formation. This heritage in infrared instrumentation is used today to train the next generation of space engineers and astronomers.
### Planck [OU 452]
OU researchers use Planck's high-resolution maps of the cosmic microwave background to study the large-scale structure of the early universe and the distribution of galaxy clusters. The university's cosmology group contributes to research into dark matter and dark energy that draws on Planck's data alongside more recent missions like Euclid. This work provides a fundamental framework for teaching cosmology and the history of the universe at the OU.
### Peregrine [OU 593]
The OU proposed and developed the Exospheric Mass Spectrometer (EMS) as a European contribution to NASA's PITMS instrument. EMS was funded by ESA and developed with RAL Space, on a fast-track process to meet tight budget and timescale constraints. Its aim was to measure the thin lunar exosphere and how it changes with both natural phenomena (such as the lunar day-night cycle) and the lander's own effects. OU researchers successfully operated the instrument in space to help diagnose the health of the spacecraft, and proved the technology ready for re-use on the upcoming PROSPECT mission.
### Comet Interceptor [OU 401]
The OU's School of Physical Sciences is a Co-Investigator institution for the DISC dust impact instrument, which will measure the size, mass and velocity of cometary dust particles during the spacecraft's high-speed flyby. The university operates a Light-Gas Gun facility, firing particles at several kilometres per second to verify the instrument's dust shield can survive real hypervelocity impacts.
### EnVision [OU 310]
The OU's Atmospheric Research and Space Exploration group leads the testing and characterisation of the UV detectors for the VenSpec-U instrument, part of the VenSpec suite, funded by a £2.3 million ESA grant. The team also takes part in science preparations for the mission ahead of launch in the early 2030s.
### LightShip-1 [OU 487]
The OU’s Atmospheric Research and Space Exploration group is leading the design phase for the High-Resolution Imager for Mars Exploration (HiRIME) telescope carried by LightShip. The OU’s work is critical to delivering high-resolution imagery of the Martian surface for planetary geology. The same group also leads the Dust Environment Spectral Imager instrument on LightShip, which will take global images of Mars about every 10 minutes to monitor the global atmosphere and dust storms.
### OGRE [OU 492]
The CEI characterises and optimises the four EM-CCD sensors in OGRE's X-ray camera, which is built by the UK company XCAM, engineered to survive the violent vibrations of a rocket launch. Academics at the university lead research into radiation damage, charge transfer efficiency, and the use of these high-speed detectors for photon counting in the soft X-ray regime. This work is part of a broader OU initiative to develop new technologies for high-energy astrophysics and stellar research.
### Hayabusa2 [OU 369]
The OU's oxygen isotope laboratory was one of the international groups invited to join "Team Kochi," one of the three Japanese-led initial analysis teams for the returned Ryugu samples. OU researchers are using the university's laboratory facilities to measure oxygen isotopes in the grains, showing that Ryugu-like material may have delivered water to the early Earth.
### OSIRIS-REx [OU 393]
OU researchers worked with NASA's OSIRIS-REx team to help select Bennu's Nightingale sample site, producing detailed temperature predictions to ensure the spacecraft and its samples didn't overheat during collection. OU academics are also involved in analysing the returned Bennu material to investigate the early Solar System's chemistry and the origin of Earth's water and organic molecules.
### Stardust [OU 383]
OU researchers were among the first scientists worldwide to analyse material from the Stardust mission, using infrared, UV/visible and Raman spectroscopy to study the mineralogy of dust grains captured from Comet Wild 2 in the aerogel collector. This work, alongside studies of the sample's organic content, helped establish the OU as a leading UK centre for cometary sample science.
### Bennu [OU 157]
OU researchers helped choose where NASA's OSIRIS-REx spacecraft would sample Bennu, and are analysing the material it brought back to Earth (see OSIRIS-REx).
### Ryugu [OU 150]
The OU's oxygen isotope laboratory is one of the international groups analysing samples of Ryugu returned by JAXA's Hayabusa2 mission (see Hayabusa2).
### Itokawa [OU 134]
OU researchers have been allocated particles of Itokawa returned by Hayabusa, to study what the asteroid is made of and how it formed.
### Hayabusa [OU 74]
OU researchers study grains of Itokawa returned by Hayabusa (see Itokawa).
### Genesis [OU 557]
OU scientists were members of the Genesis Science Team. They developed some of the detectors used to collect the solar wind, developed new techniques for analysing the returned samples, and carried out extensive work to characterise and clean up surface contamination after the capsule's crash landing on Earth. The contamination meant the OU team could not make meaningful measurements, but the mission's finding that the Sun is richer in oxygen-16 than the Earth, Moon, Mars and meteorites remains a key reference point for OU research on oxygen isotopes.
### Ariel [OU 310]
Members of the OU Astronomy group have served as science advisory team members and working group leads for Ariel, which will study the atmospheres of about 1,000 exoplanets. It will allow the first large population studies of exoplanet atmospheres, helping us understand more about how planets form and evolve.
### Open Science Observatories [OU 404]
The telescopes are owned and operated by the OU and serve as a cornerstone of its distance-learning astronomy curriculum. Academics use the facility to conduct long-term monitoring of variable stars and to provide ground-based support for international space missions like Ariel. This unique educational resource allows students to gain real-world experience in astronomical data collection and analysis.
### Mars Yard [OU 312]
The facility is integrated into the university's teaching programme, allowing students to operate robotic rovers across a simulated Martian landscape from their own homes. This innovative teaching tool is part of the OU's broad commitment to making space science accessible and interactive for a global audience.
